# AI Decision Lab — Niche Concept and Episode Bible

Status: Concept only  
Vertical: AI education / English  
Audience: managers and operators who use AI output in real work  
Production scope: documentation only

## 1. Domain boundaries

This document separates four concepts that must not be conflated:

- **Vertical:** AI education, one of the project's three existing verticals.
- **Micro-niche:** evidence-first AI decision quality for managers/operators.
- **Series:** `AI Decision Lab`, the recurring world and audience promise.
- **Micro-game:** `SHIP / FIX / STOP?`, the decision viewers make in each episode.

This is not a fourth vertical, a new Destination Channel, a new Video Type, or an accepted MAB action-space dimension.

## 2. One-sentence concept

> Managers inspect a real AI-generated work output, lock a `SHIP / FIX / STOP` decision, then watch a reproducible evidence audit reveal the correct operational verdict and its quantified consequence.

## 3. Why this niche exists

Most AI-education content teaches features, prompts, or news. `AI Decision Lab` teaches judgment:

- when an AI output is good enough to use;
- when it needs a bounded correction;
- when it is too risky or ungrounded to proceed.

The differentiating angle is not "AI makes mistakes." It is:

> **Can you correctly calibrate trust before the evidence is revealed?**

That question remains useful even as models improve.

## 4. Audience job-to-be-done

When a manager/operator is about to act on AI output, they need to decide its required review level quickly, so they can gain speed without silently transferring risk into customers, money, operations, or reputation.

Audience stakes:

1. money;
2. time;
3. reputation;
4. operational continuity;
5. customer trust.

The series deliberately avoids generic "AI tips for everyone." Every episode must have a named decision owner and an observable operational consequence.

## 5. Canonical micro-game: `SHIP / FIX / STOP?`

### Verdicts

**SHIP**  
The output has no material defect under the declared rubric and is ready for the stated use.

**FIX**  
The output is useful, but one or more bounded defects must be corrected before use.

**STOP**  
The output has a foundational, high-impact, unsafe, or unauditable failure. Editing around it would be less reliable than restarting or changing the workflow.

### Game-theory guardrail

The outcome cannot always be `STOP`, and a title cannot leak that an error definitely exists. Otherwise viewers learn a dominant strategy and stop evaluating evidence.

Required discipline:

- define the prompt and rubric before judging the output;
- preserve the unedited model response;
- do not rewrite a case backward from a desired twist;
- allow clean `SHIP` outcomes;
- allow unresolved evidence when the source record is genuinely ambiguous;
- do not impose a fixed verdict quota that overrides evidence.

## 6. Recurring world and roles

### The Manager

- owns the decision and deadline;
- wants speed and a usable answer;
- communicates business stakes in plain English;
- represents the viewer's temptation to ship.

### The AI

- presents the captured model output;
- is neither villain nor oracle;
- may be right, fixable, or materially wrong;
- must not be represented as a clone of a real person or copyrighted character.

### The Auditor

- defines the rubric;
- isolates claims/actions;
- opens primary sources and workflow evidence;
- assigns the final verdict;
- explains one portable decision rule.

These are original recurring mascot roles layered over real screen evidence. Mascots create reaction, continuity, and series recognition; they do not substitute for proof.

## 7. Visual treatment

### Primary proof surface

- real model interface or preserved output;
- readable source documents;
- spreadsheets, timelines, dashboards, or workflow states;
- captured cursor/action evidence;
- claim ledger and verdict board.

### Character layer

- original mascot overlays for Manager, AI, and Auditor;
- compact reactions near decision beats;
- no full-screen mascot sequence during a proof-critical claim;
- no copyrighted Sonic visual language or character silhouettes.

### Why not animation-only

ADR-0029 already records a known risk: animation-only or illegible terminal/presenter compositions can make evidence secondary or monotonous. `AI Decision Lab` keeps the workflow state readable and visually primary.

## 9. Canonical 50–75 second structure

Target reference: approximately 66–72 seconds. Timing is a starting hypothesis, not a hard internal split.

### 0–3s — CONSEQUENCE TEASE + DECISION

- frame 0 shows the real output and a meaningful business consequence/proof object;
- overlay: `SHIP / FIX / STOP?`;
- one-line stakes: who is about to use this, and for what;
- early SFX within 0–1s;
- no title card and no full result reveal.

Example pattern:

> "This AI forecast is about to set next month's inventory. Ship, fix, or stop?"

### 3–8s — LOCK THE CALL

- show the exact output fragment under decision;
- viewer commits mentally to one verdict;
- success rubric appears in one readable line;
- do not require a like/comment to unlock the result.

### 8–16s — ASSESS

- identify decision owner, deadline, and consequence boundary;
- count auditable claims/actions;
- show the raw before-state.

### 16–30s — ISOLATE

- split the output into at least three independently auditable checkpoints;
- close one local loop every 3–5 seconds;
- each checkpoint has a visible status: supported, weak, contradicted, or unresolved.

### 30–42s — PROCESS

- open primary sources or execute the real workflow check;
- show tool/action/result in the same beat;
- reveal the first material discrepancy or a surprising confirmation;
- keep screen text mobile-readable.

### 38–42s — INTEGRATED MID-ROLL TRIPLE CTA

Existing project policy still requires Like, Subscribe, and Comment in this window. It must be integrated into the unresolved decision, not pasted as a generic bumper.

Candidate copy pattern:

> "Lock your call: like, subscribe, and comment SHIP, FIX, or STOP before the final check."

This is a project requirement, not evidence that Zoolibeer's `HELP ME` card caused virality.

### 42–56s — BUILD

- correct the bounded defect or demonstrate why correction is insufficient;
- update the claim ledger visibly;
- accelerate state changes relative to the earlier audit;
- show audited before/after output.

### 56–66s — CONSEQUENCE SIMULATION

- label `SIMULATION` visibly;
- show the input quantities and arithmetic;
- convert the defect into time, cost, exposure, or operational impact;
- do not imply the simulated incident happened.

### 66–72s — VERDICT + PORTABLE RULE + BOARD

- reveal `SHIP`, `FIX`, or `STOP`;
- explain one transferable decision criterion;
- add the completed case to the series board;
- show the next empty category/slot without promising a fabricated result.

## 10. Evidence and truth gates

Every episode must preserve:

1. exact prompt;
2. model/provider and version/date;
3. raw model output;
4. declared intended use;
5. scoring rubric defined before verdict;
6. public/reproducible source corpus where possible;
7. primary-source preference;
8. claim ledger with supported/weak/contradicted/unresolved states;
9. consequence inputs and arithmetic;
10. final verdict with rationale.

Blocking failures:

- fabricated terminal, benchmark, source, screenshot, or incident;
- unlabeled simulation;
- verdict chosen before evidence collection;
- title claiming an error reduction or dollar loss not produced by the run;
- citation count used as a proxy for truth;
- unresolved evidence forced into true/false;
- screen evidence unreadable at mobile size;
- mascot reaction covering the proof;
- medical, legal, financial, or employment advice presented beyond the evidence and disclosure scope.

## 11. Case eligibility gate

A candidate case is eligible only when all are true:

1. **Immediate decision:** a manager/operator can understand what may be shipped within three seconds.
2. **Real run:** the output comes from a captured model execution.
3. **Auditable surface:** at least three checkpoints can be verified visually.
4. **Material stakes:** a wrong decision has a specific time, money, reputation, customer, or operational effect.
5. **Readable proof:** decisive evidence fits a phone screen after editing.
6. **Verdict uncertainty:** `SHIP`, `FIX`, and `STOP` are all plausible before the audit.
7. **Portable lesson:** the final rule generalizes beyond the exact tool or prompt.
8. **No forced twist:** the episode remains useful if the AI is correct.

Reject cases that rely on hidden domain expertise for the before/after to matter, require a long legal/medical disclaimer to be safe, or have no visible evidence beyond narrator opinion.

## 12. Labeled Counterfactual Simulation

A consequence simulation is allowed only when:

- `SIMULATION` stays visible for the whole simulated beat;
- each input quantity is sourced or explicitly declared as an assumption;
- the arithmetic is inspectable;
- a range replaces false precision when inputs are uncertain;
- no real company/person is implied to have suffered the event;
- the simulation answers the declared decision, not a sensational unrelated scenario.

Good:

> `SIMULATION — 4 agents × 2 hours × $42/hour = $336 rework`

Bad:

> `This AI mistake cost the company $12,000` when no such incident occurred.

## 13. Series continuity device

### Decision Board

A persistent board tracks completed audits by category:

- Customer communication
- Research and citations
- Forecasting
- Operations
- Data analysis
- Vendor selection
- Meeting/action tracking
- Policy/process writing

Each completed episode fills one card with:

- case name;
- model/date;
- verdict;
- dominant failure or confirmation mode.

Empty slots visualize future work, adapting the collection-board mechanic from the Jack Craig case without copying its pet-grooming surface.

## 14. Seed case backlog

These are research prompts, not predetermined verdicts or authorized productions.

| Case question | Intended decision | Proof surface | Potential stakes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can this AI-written refund response be sent? | Customer email | policy text + email diff | refunds, trust, escalation |
| Can this demand forecast set inventory? | Forecast approval | spreadsheet formulas + source data | stockout/overstock |
| Can this meeting summary assign owners? | Action-plan approval | transcript + task board | missed delivery |
| Can this vendor comparison support procurement? | Vendor shortlist | source ledger + conflict check | spend, lock-in |
| Can this research brief go to leadership? | Brief approval | claim ledger + primary sources | reputation, bad decision |
| Can this dashboard explanation be published? | KPI narrative | cohort/denominator check | wrong intervention |
| Can this AI-generated SOP be adopted? | Process change | step-by-step dry run | operational failure |
| Can this customer list be used for outreach? | Campaign launch | consent/filter/query audit | compliance, trust |
| Can this incident summary close the ticket? | Incident closure | logs/timeline + missing evidence | recurrence |
| Can this pricing analysis change the plan? | Pricing decision | assumptions + sensitivity table | revenue/margin |

No backlog item may claim a likely verdict until its controlled run and rubric evaluation exist.

## 15. Packaging grammar

### Title family

Use one decision plus one object. Do not reveal the verdict.

Patterns:

- `SHIP, FIX, OR STOP?`
- `Would You Ship This Forecast?`
- `This AI Brief Goes to the CEO`
- `Approve This AI Email?`

Final production titles would still need to pass ADR-0034's canonical metadata gate. These are concept patterns, not upload-ready titles.

### Hook grammar

- show the output and stakes together;
- withhold the decisive defect/confirmation;
- avoid generic "AI made a shocking mistake" phrasing;
- do not name both cause and specific effect if doing so resolves the gap;
- viewer must be able to state the decision after seeing only 0–3 seconds.

### Ending grammar

The ending must provide:

1. verdict;
2. decisive evidence;
3. one portable rule;
4. one earned callback or consequence;
5. Decision Board update.

No generic motivational quote and no unseeded destruction gag.

## 16. Base quality and project compatibility

Any future artifact remains subject to existing project policy:

- 9:16, 1080×1920, 50–75 seconds, H.264 with audio;
- HEIT-compatible educational payload;
- caption visible immediately and readable on mobile;
- visual change/meaningful motion every 1–2 seconds in the hook;
- proof-coupled visuals, not decorative b-roll;
- early SFX;
- Mid-Roll Triple CTA at 38–42 seconds;
- moving watermark;
- real MP4 media QC rather than new Python-renderer unit tests;
- plateau-gated publishing and 48-hour measurement if production is ever authorized.

The source videos' 9–25 second runtimes do not supersede these rules.

## 17. Anti-patterns

Never:

- use Sonic or another borrowed character universe as the cast;
- call the niche `AI Error Detective` or otherwise reveal that failure is guaranteed;
- force every episode to end `STOP`;
- use like/comment engagement as a magical rescue mechanic;
- slow a 12-second gag to 60 seconds;
- animate fake proof when a real screen can be captured;
- present counterfactual damage as a historical incident;
- make the AI a permanent villain;
- choose a verdict for emotional impact before the audit;
- use unreadable terminal/code screens for a manager audience;
- launch a fourth vertical or channel under this concept without a separate decision.

## 18. Future validation path — documented, not authorized

If the user later reopens production, the least-risk path is:

1. choose one eligible controlled case;
2. preserve prompt/output/rubric/source corpus;
3. script a 50–75 second episode using this bible;
4. build one non-uploaded prototype;
5. run full media/trust QC;
6. only after explicit approval, design an AB Experiment that changes one narrative variable.
